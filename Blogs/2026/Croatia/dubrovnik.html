🏰 Dubrovnik & the Southern Coast

Days 1–3 • April 25–27, 2026 • Pearl of the Adriatic

Day 1 — The Adriatic Approach
Saturday, April 25 • Pelješac Peninsula & the Southern Dalmatian Coast
👣 10,865 steps 🥾 5.1 miles 🏢 19 floors

Croatia announced itself before we'd even left the airport road. The drive from Dubrovnik Airport along the southern Dalmatian coast gave us our first look at what the next ten days held — pale limestone plunging to water that couldn't decide whether to be turquoise or cobalt, both options glittering equally under the April sun. We'd seen Adriatic photographs. None of them had prepared us for the actual colour.

The route north from the airport passes through one of the most dramatic coastal stretches in the Mediterranean. The Pelješac Peninsula — a 65-kilometre spit of limestone, pine, and vineyard jutting out from the mainland — forms the southern threshold of central Dalmatia. Here, Croatia's finest red wines grow on near-vertical slopes above the sea: the Dingač and Postup appellations, where Plavac Mali grapes cling to gradients so steep that harvesting is done by hand, and sometimes by boat.

We stopped on the peninsula's coastal road and watched the Pelješac Channel glitter between the mainland and the cape. The water changes colour by the minute depending on depth and light — shallow flats glowing an almost electric turquoise, deeper channels dropping to deep indigo. The Dalmatian coast is everything the postcards claim, and then considerably more.

Pelješac Peninsula: Croatia's second-longest peninsula extends 65km into the Adriatic and is home to the country's most prestigious red wine appellations: Dingač and Postup. The peninsula also contains Ston, a 14th-century salt-production town with 5.5km of walls — the longest in Europe after the Great Wall of China. The 2.4km Pelješac Bridge (opened 2022) now connects the peninsula directly to the mainland, bypassing the narrow Bosnia and Herzegovina coastal corridor.

Day 1 Evening — First Steps in the Old Town
Saturday, April 25 • Through the Pile Gate at Dusk

We checked into Sun Gardens Dubrovnik in the village of Orašac — a five-star Leading Hotels of the World property perched on coastal slopes fifteen minutes north of the Old Town, with a private pebble beach and four pools set into natural Mediterranean woodland. The drive in from the airport had already given us our first glimpse of the fortified city rising from the sea — all red rooftops, pale stone towers, and crystalline water — and the jet lag had evaporated somewhere along the Pelješac coast road.

That first evening we took the hotel shuttle into the Old Town. Walking through the Pile Gate for the first time as dusk settled over the Stradun was quietly overwhelming. The great limestone promenade glows amber at golden hour; the flagstones underfoot are polished mirror-smooth by eight centuries of continuous footfall; the Adriatic winks at the end of every side alley. We ate well at a table on the Stradun, watched the light fade from honey to copper to a deep violet, and went to bed early.

The next morning, properly rested, we set out to earn the views.

Sun Gardens Dubrovnik: A Leading Hotels of the World property with a private pebble beach, four pools (one indoor), and a complimentary shuttle to the Old Town. The position above the coast, set into natural Mediterranean woodland just north of the city, makes for one of the most beautiful hotel settings in the region. The views from the terraced gardens over the sea and the distant Elafiti islands are reason alone to stay.
Day 2 — The City Walls
Sunday, April 26 • A 2km Circuit Above the Rooftops
👣 23,991 steps 🥾 11.3 miles 🏢 86 floors — the trip's highest single day

The city walls of Dubrovnik are without qualification the most impressive medieval fortifications in the Mediterranean. Not the most dramatic — those might be Carcassonne or the Alhambra — but here, uniquely, the walls are still completely intact, still encircle the entire city, and still stand between 4 and 6 metres thick. The Old Town they enclose has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1979 and looks essentially as it did six hundred years ago.

The 2km wall walk takes you around the full perimeter at height, offering a relentless sequence of views that switch between two completely different worlds: on one side, the dense terracotta-tile roofscape of the Old Town, packed with Baroque churches, Romanesque towers, and Renaissance palaces; on the other, the open Adriatic dropping straight from the cliff face below. The sea was doing its best Adriatic impression — deep sapphire in the offshore depths, lightening to clear cobalt near the rocky shoreline.

At the western end of the walk, Fort Bokar marks the point where the walls meet the sea cliff, and the views south along the coast open up suddenly: the island of Lokrum a kilometre offshore, the Elafiti chain dissolving into haze to the north, and the fortified peninsula of the Old Town framed between sea and sky. We walked slowly and photographed freely.

The City Walls: Dubrovnik's walls were built and rebuilt between the 12th and 17th centuries, with their current form largely dating to the 14th–15th century. The walls are 1,940m long, reinforced by 3 circular forts, 14 square towers, and 2 corner towers. They survived centuries of Ottoman siege, a devastating 1667 earthquake, and the 1991–92 Yugoslav Army bombardment — during which all rooftops visible from the photos below were destroyed and subsequently rebuilt in identical replacement terracotta tiles.

Day 2–3 — Lovrijenac Fort & Panoramic Views
April 26–27 • Above the Old Town

Lovrijenac Fort stands on a 37-metre sea cliff half a kilometre west of the Pile Gate, connected to the main city by a narrow bridge over the sea. The fort is entirely free-standing — not attached to the main walls — and was built by the Ragusan Republic in 1018 to prevent the Venetians from occupying the strategic headland. Above the inner gate, the inscription that has stood for a thousand years: Non bene pro toto libertas venditur auro — Freedom cannot be sold for all the gold in the world. The Ragusan Republic took its independence seriously.

From the fort's upper battlements, looking back east toward the Old Town, the view resolves into something closer to an architectural model than a real city: the walled peninsula surrounded entirely by deep blue water, the rooftops within the walls uniform and ordered, every bell tower and dome identified. Looking south from the same spot, the coastline continues toward Montenegro in a series of rocky headlands and coves.

The elevated viewpoints above the northern wall give the most complete overview of Dubrovnik — the full relationship between the fortified peninsula and its maritime setting. From here the island of Lokrum is clearly visible less than a kilometre offshore, the Elafiti archipelago stretches away to the northwest, and the open southern Adriatic disappears toward the horizon. It is one of those views that explains immediately why this particular strip of coast has been continuously fought over, traded, and celebrated for two and a half thousand years.

Lovrijenac Fort: Built in 1018 when a Venetian fleet arrived with materials to build a fort — the Ragusans constructed their own in three months before the Venetians could land. The fort was so strategically important that the Republic required its commanders to swear each month that they would surrender it to no man, not even the Rector of Ragusa, on pain of death. Today it hosts the Dubrovnik Summer Festival's performances of Hamlet each year — the battlements making a fittingly dramatic stage.
