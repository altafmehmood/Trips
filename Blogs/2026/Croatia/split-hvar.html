🏛️ Makarska & Split

Days 3–5 • April 27–29, 2026 • Riviera to Roman Palace

Day 3: The Makarska Riviera
Monday, April 27 • Driving North from Dubrovnik
👣 13,392 steps 🥾 6.3 miles 🏢 10 floors

We left Dubrovnik early on Day 3 and headed north up the Magistrala, the ancient coastal highway that has threaded its way between cliff and sea for two thousand years. The drive is spectacular from the first kilometre: the Adriatic drops away to the right while the limestone massifs rise to the left, the road carved directly into the cliff face in places, hugging the contour of the coast with the confidence of a road that has been here since Roman times.

The Makarska Riviera begins about 60km north of Dubrovnik, where the coast bends west and the Biokovo mountain range suddenly fills the windscreen with a scale that makes you involuntarily ease off the accelerator. The mountains rise from beach level to 1,762 metres in the space of about two kilometres horizontal distance, one of the steepest coastal mountain walls anywhere in the Mediterranean. The effect is a landscape of spectacular compression: pebble beaches at sea level, pine and cypress halfway up, bare white rock and then snow above.

Makarska itself is a compact resort town tucked into a bay between two limestone headlands, the old quarter arranged around a pleasant main square and a harbour backed by the Biokovo massif. We walked the seafront, swam in the extraordinarily clear water, and ate lunch at a table directly on the waterfront, the mountains watching from behind in complete silence. The light in the Adriatic at midday is almost blinding: the white limestone and the blue sea reflect it between them until the air itself seems to glow.

Biokovo Mountain: The Biokovo massif rises to 1,762m at Sveti Jure, Croatia's second-highest peak, just 2km from the Makarska waterfront. The mountain is a protected nature park with some of the most dramatic hiking in Croatia, views extending to Italy on clear days. The Biokovo Skywalk, a glass-bottomed observation platform cantilevered over the cliff at 1,228m, opened in 2021.

Days 4–5: Split: Living Inside History
April 28–29 • Diocletian's Palace & the Riva
👣 21,151 steps 🥾 10.0 miles 🏢 59 floors

Split does something that very few cities manage: it makes you feel the full weight of history not in a museum, not behind glass, but underfoot and in the walls around you. Diocletian's Palace was built between 295 and 305 AD as the retirement complex of the Roman Emperor Diocletian: 30,000 square metres of fortified palace occupying the entire southeastern quarter of the modern city. The twist is that it never became a ruin. When Diocletian died in 313 AD, local people moved in, subdivided the rooms, opened shops in the corridors, and the palace became a city. It has been continuously inhabited for seventeen hundred years.

Walking the narrow lanes inside the palace walls today, you brush past Roman columns that support Croatian apartment buildings, duck under 4th-century stone arches to reach a bar terrace, and emerge suddenly into the Peristyle, the colonnaded central courtyard of the imperial complex, to find it still serving its original function as the heart of the city's social life. People sit on the Roman steps eating ice cream. Locals cut through the temple courtyard on their way to the market. The past and present have been so thoroughly interwoven here that the distinction has largely ceased to matter.

The Cathedral of Saint Domnius deserves particular attention: Diocletian built it as his own mausoleum. In the 7th century, Croatian Christians converted it into a cathedral dedicated to a bishop who Diocletian himself had martyred. The irony has stood for 1,400 years. The octagonal building, its 12th-century Romanesque bell tower rising beside it, remains one of the oldest continuously used cathedral buildings in the world.

The Riva promenade runs along the southern harbour front, lined with palms and cafe chairs, the sea wall of Diocletian's Palace rising directly behind the waterfront terraces. We sat here at dusk and watched the ferries depart for the Dalmatian islands: Hvar, Brač, Vis. The sun turned the limestone a shade of warm gold that photographs never quite capture. Split earns its second look.

Diocletian's Palace: Built 295–305 AD as the retirement complex of Emperor Diocletian, the only Roman emperor to voluntarily abdicate. The compound covers 3 hectares and once housed around 9,000 people. Today the palace walls contain dozens of streets, restaurants, shops and apartments, along with three UNESCO-listed sites. Approximately 3,000 people live inside the ancient walls: the world's most densely inhabited archaeological site.
