🇸🇮 Slovenia & Zagreb

Days 8–10 • May 1–3, 2026 • Soča Valley, Lake Bled & the Croatian Capital

Day 8 — Into Slovenia: The Soča Valley
Saturday, May 2 • Triglav National Park & the Emerald River

Leaving Plitvice on Day 8, we drove northwest through Croatia's Lika region toward the Slovenian border, the landscape shifting gradually from the karst plateau we'd crossed two days earlier toward the foothills of the Julian Alps. The Croatian-Slovenian border crossing is unremarkable — a brief formality on the way to somewhere extraordinary.

The road into Slovenia drops down toward the Soča Valley, and the Soča River appears suddenly in a gap in the trees — and stops you completely. The Soča is one of the most beautiful rivers in Europe. Its colour is unlike anything else in the Alps or the Mediterranean: a deep, luminous emerald-turquoise produced by the interaction of glacial meltwater from the Triglav glaciers and the fine calcium carbonate sediment of the Julian Alps limestone. Other rivers are turquoise. The Soča is a different colour entirely — something between emerald, jade, and glacier-melt, shot through with a luminosity that makes it look lit from below even on overcast days.

We drove through the Soča Valley inside Triglav National Park — Slovenia's only national park, protecting 880 sq km of the Julian Alps including the 2,864m summit of Triglav itself, Slovenia's highest peak and national symbol. The valley road winds above the river through a gorge of extraordinary vertical limestone, the Soča visible in flashes below through the canyon walls. We stopped wherever the road allowed and looked down at water that required several attempts to accept as naturally occurring.

The Soča River: Rising at 1,058m in the cirque below Triglav's northern face, the Soča flows 138km southwest through Slovenia and into Italy (where it becomes the Isonzo). The extraordinary emerald colour is produced by the combination of glacial mineral content — very fine calcium carbonate — and the reflective white limestone riverbed. The Soča was the site of eleven brutal battles during World War I (the Isonzo Front, 1915–17), in which nearly 300,000 soldiers died in the narrow Alpine valley. The Hemingway novel A Farewell to Arms is set here.

Day 8 — Lake Bled: Slovenia's Fairy-Tale
Saturday, May 2 • Island Church, Clifftop Castle & the Julian Alps

Lake Bled is a place that you think you already know from photographs, and then you arrive and find that the photographs are inadequate. Not wrong — the island church is there, the clifftop castle is there, the Julian Alps are there — but the photographs cannot convey the scale relationship between the elements, or the quality of the light on the water, or the silence on the lake before the day-trip coaches arrive at 9am.

The lake is 2km long and 1.4km wide, formed when a glacier retreated at the end of the last ice age and left a natural karst depression filled with spring water. In the centre of the lake, an island — the only natural island in Slovenia — topped by a 17th-century Baroque church whose bell tower rings across the water on every quiet morning. On a cliff 130 metres above the lake's northern shore, Bled Castle, first documented in 1011 AD and continuously occupied for a thousand years.

We took a Pletna — the traditional flat-bottomed wooden boat propelled by a single standing oarsman — to the island. The 99 stone steps from the island's landing up to the church door are traditionally climbed by the groom carrying the bride in his arms on a Slovenian wedding day. At the church, a wishing bell hangs in the tower. You ring it three times and make a wish. We rang it, made our wishes, and took the boat back across the lake in the late afternoon sun with the Julian Alps filling the northern sky from one shore to the other.

The castle walk in the evening, after most day-trippers had left, gave us the full panoramic view of the lake from 130 metres above: the island in the centre of the oval lake, the Julian Alps as a snow-capped semicircle, the lake itself shifting from deep teal near the island to pale jade green near the shallows. It is one of the best views in Europe. We stayed until the light was gone.

Lake Bled: The lake sits at 475m in the Julian Alps foothills. The island church (Church of the Assumption of Mary) has been a pilgrimage site since the 8th century; the current Baroque structure dates to 1698. The Pletna boats are a protected traditional craft — the right to operate them has been hereditary in Bled families since the 17th century. Bled Castle (Blejski Grad) dates to at least 1004 AD and is the oldest castle in Slovenia. The Bled cream cake (kremna rezina), invented here in 1953, is protected by a geographic designation — officially, it can only be made in Bled.

Days 9–10 — Zagreb: Croatia's Capital
May 2–3 • Upper Town, Dolac Market & the Final Farewell

We drove from Lake Bled to Zagreb on the afternoon of Day 9, crossing back into Croatia through the Slovenian-Croatian border at Bregana and arriving in the Croatian capital in time for a late dinner. Zagreb had always been the final act of the trip — the departure city, the loose end to be tied before the airport — and we'd allocated a full Day 10 to explore it properly rather than treating it as a transit stop.

Zagreb divides into two distinct worlds. The Upper Town (Gornji Grad) occupies the twin medieval hills of Gradec and Kaptol, connected by the famous Tkalčićeva pedestrian street lined with cafe terraces. The Lower Town (Donji Grad) is the orderly Austro-Hungarian grid laid out in the 19th century, with grand boulevards and parks in the Ringstrasse tradition. The two halves connect via the world's shortest funicular railway, 66 metres long, operating continuously since 1890.

We started at Dolac, Zagreb's celebrated outdoor market, which occupies a terrace above Ban Jelačić Square. The red umbrella canopies of the market stalls are Zagreb's most photographed view from above: a sea of overlapping crimson circles against the stone below, the traders selling fruit, vegetables, cheese, and flowers from dawn until early afternoon. We ate cheese and fruit from the market on the terrace steps and watched the city wake up.

The Upper Town deserves a full morning: the Church of St Mark with its uniquely multicoloured tiled roof showing the coats of arms of medieval Croatia and Zagreb; Lotrščak Tower (13th century) from which a cannon is fired at noon every day, a tradition maintained without interruption since 1877; the Parliament and Government buildings clustered in the narrow streets of Gradec. From the Lotrščak Tower observation deck, the Lower Town spreads south in a perfect grid toward the Sava River, the whole city visible in a single glance.

The twin-spired Cathedral of Saint Domnius — identical name to Split's cathedral, no relation — rises 105 metres above the Lower Town and is visible from much of the city. The 19th-century neo-Gothic spires are a later addition; the cathedral body is substantially older, partially damaged in the 1880 earthquake and subsequently rebuilt. Walking back from the cathedral to the airport in the late afternoon, Zagreb felt like a proper capital city that had been undersold by most itineraries: it deserved more time than we'd given it, and more time than most visitors give it.

Zagreb: Croatia's capital (population 860,000) has been continuously settled since Roman times as Andautonia. The modern city formed from the merger of the twin medieval hill-towns of Gradec (founded 1242) and Kaptol (founded c.1094). The Lotrščak Tower cannon has been fired at noon every day since 1877 without a single day's interruption. Zagreb has more museums per capita than any other European capital. The Dolac market has operated on its current site since 1930.

We flew home from Zagreb Airport on Day 10: British Airways BA 849 (A320neo) to London Heathrow, then BA 197 (787-9) back to Houston IAH. Ten days in two countries, three national parks, three UNESCO World Heritage sites, approximately 520 miles of driving, and more turquoise water than we knew what to do with.

Zagreb in the rearview mirror, the Medvednica hills behind it, and then the Slovenian and Croatian karst dissolving into cloud. We'd go back tomorrow.
