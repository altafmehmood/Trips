Day 5: Krka National Park
Wednesday, April 29 • Skradinski Buk at Spring Peak Flow
👣 18,049 steps 🥾 8.5 miles 🏢 13 floors

We arrived at Krka National Park in the morning and honestly, the hype had been building for days. We had spent an evening in Dubrovnik scrolling through photos of Skradinski Buk on travel blogs, and yaar, nothing had actually prepared us for it.

Krka works on the same geological principle as Plitvice. Mineral-rich water slowly deposits travertine barriers, and behind those barriers the river pools into luminous turquoise basins. The Krka River comes rolling out of the Dinara mountains through a 45km limestone canyon, and at Skradinski Buk it just spreads across the full 800-metre width of the valley in 17 separate waterfall streams threading between tufa islands, dropping 45 metres in total. There is honestly no appropriate response to seeing Skradinski Buk for the first time. You just sort of stop and stare.

We were lucky to visit in late April, which is peak flow. The snowmelt from the Dinara range is still feeding the river at full volume. Locals told us summer flows can be half of what we were seeing. That explains everything. The mist was carrying 50 metres upwind, the sound hit you in the chest, and the colour of the water, I cannot explain the colour of this water. That turquoise is real. No filter. No editing. It just looks like that.

The boardwalk takes you right into the falls, within metres of the water at multiple points. Obviously we had to stop at every single crossing for photos. We stayed three hours. We left only because Zadar was waiting and the sun does not negotiate on timing.

Krka National Park: Established 1985, protecting 142 sq km of the Krka River canyon. There are 7 major waterfalls inside, the most famous being Skradinski Buk (45m) and Roški Slap (22m). The travertine formations grow at roughly 1cm per year as mineral-rich water deposits calcium carbonate. The park has been UNESCO World Heritage nominated. And if you're planning to go: swimming in the waterfalls was banned from 2021 to protect the travertine. So no, you cannot jump in, even though every part of you will want to.

En Route: The Zrmanja Canyon at Maslenica
April 29 • A Hidden Canyon Off the Highway

Driving north from Krka to Zadar, the route drops you past one of Croatia's most spectacular landscapes that almost nobody stops for. The Zrmanja River has spent millennia carving through the Velebit limestone here, creating a 25-kilometre gorge with walls over 200 metres above the emerald river below. Most travellers see it from the motorway bridge for about three seconds and then the road curves away. We pulled over.

The colour of the Zrmanja is distinct from anything at Krka or Plitvice. It has a deeper forest-green quality, reflecting the heavy woodland above and the high canyon walls. Standing at the bridge railing looking straight down into the gorge is genuinely a bit vertiginous. It is one of Croatia's most impressive natural landscapes, and it has essentially zero tourist infrastructure because everyone is in a hurry to get somewhere else.

The canyon was found by kayakers decades before any tourist development arrived, and it's still primarily a white-water sports destination. That is the whole point of pulling over. Croatia keeps hiding things in plain sight like this.

The Zrmanja River Canyon: The Zrmanja rises in the Lika highlands and descends 89km to the sea near Novigrad. The lower 25km form one of Croatia's most dramatic gorge landscapes, with canyon walls to 200m and a river known for its extraordinary emerald colour. The canyon is Croatia's premier kayaking destination, the annual Zrmanja Race, held since 1982, is one of the oldest kayak races in the former Yugoslavia.

Day 5 Evening: Zadar & the Adriatic Sunset
Wednesday, April 29 • Sea Organ & the World's Best Sunset

Alfred Hitchcock visited Zadar in 1964 and called the sunset here "the most beautiful in the world." We arrived in mid-afternoon with enough time to walk the old town, find the sea organ, and position ourselves on the western waterfront for what the sun was promising to deliver.

Zadar is genuinely underrated. It has 3,000 years of continuous habitation, more classical monuments per square metre than anywhere in Croatia outside Split, and a Roman Forum that was only partially excavated in the 20th century. It is the kind of old that Dubrovnik charges admission to be near. But the thing that caught us completely off-guard was the Sea Organ. In 2005, architect Nikola Bašić cut 35 channels under the waterfront steps and connected them to organ pipes below. Every movement of the waves forces air through a different combination of pipes, creating a continuous, unpredictable, harmonically correct chord that plays without interruption as long as the sea moves. We sat on those steps for thirty minutes just listening. No music, no performance, no musician. Just the sea playing itself. Honestly weird and completely wonderful.

Then the sun started dropping behind the Kornati island chain, and Hitchcock's claim started to look less like a tourist brochure. The geography is what makes this sunset work: the sea opens to the west toward a horizon of distant islands, and the sun descends through that layered distance in sequence. Sea, then islands, then more sea, then more islands, then the horizon. Each layer does something different to the light. The result is a sunset that builds in stages rather than all at once, with new bands of colour appearing as the sun tracks lower. By the time the sky went deep violet and the first stars came out, we had been sitting on those sea steps for well over an hour. We would have stayed longer but we were getting hungry.

The Sea Organ (Morske Orgulje): Designed by architect Nikola Bašić and opened in 2005, the Sea Organ consists of 35 polyethylene tubes built under the marble steps of Zadar's western seafront. The tubes are connected to seven sections of five organ pipes each. The rising and falling of the sea pushes and pulls air through the pipes in constantly changing combinations, producing a continuous, harmonically consonant musical performance. No two moments are identical. Bašić designed it as a meditation on the relationship between human creativity and natural forces, and also won the European Prize for Urban Public Space for it in 2006.

Day 6 Morning: Paklenica NP & the Velebit Highlands
Thursday, April 30 • Canyon Hike into the Velebit, then North toward Plitvice
👣 12,927 steps 🥾 6.1 miles 🏢 38 floors. Paklenica canyon climb

Before pointing north toward Plitvice, we made a morning stop that the itinerary hadn't formally planned but the geography made irresistible: Paklenica National Park, where the Velebit range splits open along two spectacular limestone canyons that pour down from the mountains to the coast road. The entrance is right off the D8 coastal highway, about 40km north of Zadar. You'd have to actively ignore it to drive past.

Paklenica is one of Croatia's oldest national parks, established in 1949, built around two parallel gorges that have split the southern Velebit range open like someone took a cleaver to it. Velika Paklenica is the main event: 14km long, walls reaching 400 metres on either side. We entered through the main gate and the walls closed in almost immediately. Rock climbing routes cover every available surface. Paklenica is one of the premier climbing destinations in the Mediterranean, with over 150 established routes on the dolomitic limestone, and the scale of the walls is such that you feel both intimate with the rock and completely dwarfed at the same time. Walking the canyon floor with those 400-metre walls overhead is a different feeling from anything at Krka or Zadar.

The stream runs along the canyon floor, with the path crossing it on wooden bridges as the forest changes with elevation: pine giving way to beech as you gain height. About halfway up, a tunnel entrance in the rock marks the WWII underground bunker Tito had blasted into the mountain, now a mountaineering centre. We turned back here rather than push to the summit plateau, Plitvice was waiting and we had a long drive ahead. The 38 floors on the pedometer tell the story of how seriously Paklenica takes the business of altitude.

Back on the coastal road, we turned inland and began the drive north through the Velebit foothills, one of Croatia's least-visited and most dramatically austere landscapes. The road climbs through sinkholes, limestone pavements, and scrubland shaped entirely by water dissolving rock over millions of years, the same geological process that created Krka and Plitvice, but here visible at its raw, undecorated source. The emptiness felt earned: after days of crowds at Dubrovnik, Split, and Krka, the Velebit hinterland felt like Croatia breathing out.

Paklenica National Park: Established 1949: Croatia's second-oldest national park. Part of the Velebit UNESCO Biosphere Reserve. Velika Paklenica gorge: 14km long, walls up to 400m. Over 150 sport climbing routes on dolomitic limestone. Griffon vulture colony resident on the upper cliffs. WWII underground bunker at mid-canyon (now a mountaineering centre). Velebit range: Croatia's largest mountain system, 145km long, rising to 1,758m.
