🏔️ The Three National Parks

Croatia stacks three of its eight national parks along this exact road-trip corridor — each one operating on a completely different geological logic

💦
Krka National Park
Est. 1985 · Day 5 · 109 km²

Krka's defining feature is its travertine — a porous limestone deposited by mineral-rich water over thousands of years, forming natural dams and terraced cascades along 73km of river canyon. The showpiece is Skradinski Buk: seventeen separate travertine steps dropping 45 metres in total, through which the river splits into dozens of white threads before pooling into luminous turquoise basins. In spring, snowmelt from the Dinaric Alps pushes the flow to its thundering peak. The park also contains the 15th-century Franciscan monastery on Visovac island, sitting improbably in the middle of the widened river lake.

Skradinski Buk Travertine Cascades Visovac Monastery
🧗
Paklenica National Park
Est. 1949 · Day 6 · Velebit Massif

Where Krka is all water and softness, Paklenica is vertical limestone and raw altitude. The park cuts into the Velebit mountain range — Croatia's longest and the backbone of the Dinaric Alps — through two dramatic gorges carved by torrential rivers now reduced to seasonal streams. Velika Paklenica gorge runs 14km with walls rising 400 metres on either side; its sheer cliff faces host over 150 rock-climbing routes up to grade 8a, making it one of Europe's premier climbing destinations. The canyon floor hides a WWII partisan bunker, blasted into the rock in 1943. Paklenica sits within the larger Velebit UNESCO Biosphere Reserve, and griffon vultures ride the thermals overhead.

Velika Paklenica Gorge 150+ Climbing Routes WWII Bunker
🏞️
Plitvice Lakes National Park
Est. 1949 · Day 7 · UNESCO 1979

The most visited park in Croatia, and deservedly so. Sixteen terraced lakes cascade one into the next over a series of travertine barriers built over millennia by the same mineral deposition process at work in Krka — but here at a scale that overwhelms. The water's extraordinary teal colour comes from dissolved calcium carbonate interacting with mosses, algae, and specific cyanobacteria that form the travertine itself; the exact shade shifts minute to minute as cloud and sun rebalance the surface. Veliki Slap, at 78 metres, is Croatia's tallest waterfall and roars at its most dramatic after spring snowmelt. The park receives over 1.2 million visitors annually; arriving at opening time in late April, with the spring flow at full volume and no crowds, felt like having it to ourselves.

Final Reflections

Croatia has a way of exceeding even generous expectations. We arrived primed for the coastline and the history — we didn't anticipate quite how completely the national parks would steal the show. But nothing — not the research, not the travel writing, not other people's photographs — prepared us for the reality of Krka and Plitvice. Both parks were visited at peak spring flow with snowmelt from the Dinarics feeding every cascade to its maximum, and both were, simply, mind-blowing. We've looked back through the photos since returning and they still don't fully capture it: the water is an almost hallucinogenic shade of teal that your brain keeps trying to attribute to a filter or a trick of the light. It isn't. That is the actual colour, in real life, in front of you. Standing at the edge of Skradinski Buk or looking down the chain of Plitvice's sixteen lakes from the upper viewpoint are the kinds of moments that recalibrate what you think nature is capable of producing.

Dubrovnik, for all the tourist noise surrounding it, still managed to deliver moments of pure magic. Watching the sun slide into the Adriatic from the summit of Mount Srđ — the walled city glowing amber below, the Elafiti islands dissolving into crimson haze — is the kind of view that makes you understand why people call this place extraordinary. Walking the city walls at golden hour, with the limestone towers and terracotta rooftops on one side and open sea on the other, remains a highlight of the entire trip.

Slovenia was the unexpected bonus. Lake Bled is every bit the fairy-tale it promises: the island church, the clifftop castle, the Pletna rowing boats, the Julian Alps reflected in emerald water. We squeezed in a detour to Lake Bohinj and found it even more quietly beautiful — bigger, wilder, and almost entirely free of the crowds. The drive through the Alps from Plitvice to Bled through spring-green mountain passes was itself one of the day's highlights.

Ten days was enough to feel the shape of these two countries without rushing. We'd go back tomorrow.
