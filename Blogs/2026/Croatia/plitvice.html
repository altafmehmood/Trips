🏞️ Plitvice Lakes

Days 7–8 • April 30–May 1, 2026 • Croatia's Crown Jewel

Day 6 Afternoon: The Approach through Lika
Thursday, April 30 • Through the Karst Forest to Plitvice

After leaving the Velebit highlands, the road to Plitvice climbs into Lika County, Croatia's central highland region. Dense beech and fir forest, limestone sinkholes, meadows, and the occasional village. The area was heavily depopulated during the 1991-95 war and it still feels emptier than it has any right to be. Long stretches of pristine primeval forest with no settlement visible in any direction. It felt remote in a way that reminded us of the road up to Chopta in Uttarakhand, honestly. Just trees and silence and mist.

Before Plitvice, the town of Slunj appears. It sits at the confluence of the Korana and Slunjčica rivers and has its own miniature waterfall scene called Rastoke, where old water mills straddle a series of cascades right in the town centre. Lovely in its own right, though obviously completely overshadowed by what was waiting 30km to the south. We stopped briefly, took photos of the mills and cascades, and kept moving. Priorities!

Lika County: Croatia's second-largest county by area, Lika is one of the least densely populated regions in mainland Europe, with fewer than 10 people per square kilometre. The area was at the heart of the 1991-95 war and significant depopulation occurred. Today it is recovering as a tourism destination, with Plitvice Lakes NP as its anchor. The landscape alternates between bare karst ridges and dense primeval beech-fir forest, with the large Velebit Nature Park forming its western wall.

Day 7: Plitvice: The Upper Lakes
Friday, May 1 • Into the Upper Lake System
👣 19,731 steps 🥾 9.3 miles 🏢 38 floors

We set the alarm for 5:30am for Plitvice. No complaints. This is the single most important piece of advice we can give anyone visiting this park: arrive at opening time (7am), not at 10am when you feel like it. By 9am the entrance queues are serious. By 11am the famous boardwalk sections are so busy you're shuffling in a slow crocodile. At 7am, with mist sitting on the water and absolutely no one around except birdsong? It felt like we had one of Croatia's greatest natural wonders completely to ourselves. Worth every minute of that alarm.

The park is sixteen lakes connected by waterfalls and cascades, dropping 133 metres from Prošćansko jezero at the top (636m) to Novakovića Brod at the bottom (503m). Upper Lakes (twelve of them), Lower Lakes (four), with an electric ferry crossing Lake Kozjak in the middle. 8 kilometres of trail total. We had a printed map and a plan. We stuck to it, mostly.

The upper lakes are quieter and more intimate. Smaller lakes, lower falls, the forest right up close on both sides. The water colour changes from lake to lake depending on depth and mineral content. Some are pale jade-green, some deep teal, some a blue so vivid it looks like someone turned up the saturation slider in Lightroom. They didn't. In the morning light, the shallow sections show the travertine bed below so clearly that the water appears to glow from underneath. We took more photos in two hours here than we had in the previous three days combined.

Plitvice Lakes National Park: UNESCO World Heritage since 1979. The park covers 296 sq km across the Mala Kapela and Plješevica foothills, protecting 16 lakes, 90+ waterfalls, and the Čorkova Uvala old-growth beech-fir forest (one of the last truly primeval forests in Europe). The teal colour comes from dissolved calcium carbonate and magnesium reacting with the pale travertine bed. It cannot be replicated by filters. It genuinely looks like that.

Day 7: The Lower Lakes & Veliki Slap
Friday, May 1 • Towards Croatia's Highest Waterfall

The electric ferry across Lake Kozjak to the lower section costs nothing extra and takes about ten minutes. On the other side, the character of the park changes completely. The valley opens up, the falls get bigger, and the whole thing builds toward what is coming at the southern end. By this point we had already been walking for four hours and still didn't want to stop.

Veliki Slap is Croatia's highest waterfall at 78 metres. The approach is brilliant: you hear it before you see it, then glimpse it through the trees, then suddenly you are standing at the base looking straight up at 78 metres of water in freefall. In April, with full snowmelt, the volume is at its annual peak. The entire width of the fall is a solid white curtain. The plunge pool below is churning. The mist drifts back up the cliff face and lands on your face. We stood there for a very long time and said nothing particularly useful to each other. Sometimes that is the right response.

The lower lake boardwalks are the most photographed sections in the park, and honestly, rightly so. Wide cascades framed by dense forest, turquoise pools below each fall, mist softening the light. In the afternoon the spray catches the sun and there are rainbows everywhere. We have been to a lot of beautiful places. Plitvice is something else entirely.

Veliki Slap: At 78 metres, Croatia's highest waterfall drops in a single unbroken column from the canyon rim above the Korana River gorge. It's fed by a karst spring above the rim, not a surface stream: the water pushes through porous limestone rather than flowing over a river lip. In peak spring flow (April–May), the fall width can exceed 20 metres. Below it, the Korana River gorge is itself dramatic: 40-metre limestone walls carved by the same water that built the lakes above.
