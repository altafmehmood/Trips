🏞️ Plitvice Lakes

Days 7–8 • April 30–May 1, 2026 • Croatia's Crown Jewel

Day 6 Afternoon — The Approach through Lika
Thursday, April 30 • Through the Karst Forest to Plitvice

After leaving the Velebit highlands, the road to Plitvice climbs into Lika County — Croatia's central highland region, a landscape of dense beech and fir forest broken by sinkholes, meadows, and the occasional village. The area was heavily depopulated during the 1991–95 war and still feels emptier than its natural beauty warrants: long stretches of pristine primeval forest with no settlement visible in any direction, the road cutting through old-growth woodland that has been here undisturbed for centuries.

The town of Slunj appears before Plitvice: a small town at the confluence of the Korana and Slunjčica rivers, with its own miniature version of Plitvice — the Rastoke waterfalls, where old water mills straddle a series of cascades in the centre of the town. It is a significant sight in its own right, though almost entirely overshadowed by its famous neighbour 30km to the south. We stopped briefly, photographed the mills and cascades, and continued south.

Lika County: Croatia's second-largest county by area, Lika is one of the least densely populated regions in mainland Europe — fewer than 10 people per square kilometre. The area was at the heart of the 1991–95 war and significant depopulation occurred; today it is recovering as a tourism destination, with Plitvice Lakes NP as its anchor. The landscape alternates between bare karst ridges and dense primeval beech-fir forest, with the large Velebit Nature Park forming its western wall.

Day 7 — Plitvice: The Upper Lakes
Friday, May 1 • Into the Upper Lake System

We arrived at Plitvice Lakes National Park at opening time — 7am — and had the upper lake trail almost entirely to ourselves for the first hour. This is the correct strategy. By 9am the main entrance is processing serious numbers of visitors; by 11am the boardwalks at the most-photographed sections are dense with people. At dawn, with mist still hanging over the lakes and birdsong overwhelming the sound of the water, the place has an entirely different quality.

The park is organised around a chain of sixteen lakes connected by waterfalls and cascades, descending over 133 metres from the highest upper lake (Prošćansko jezero, at 636m) to the lowest (Novakovića Brod, at 503m). The lakes are divided into the Upper Lakes (twelve) and Lower Lakes (four), with a ferry service crossing the main lake (Kozjak) between the two sections. The total system spans 8 kilometres of trail through the park's old-growth forest.

The upper lakes are the quieter, more intimate section: smaller lakes, lower falls, and the forest pressing in closely on both sides. The water colour varies between the lakes — some a pale jade-green, others a deeper teal — depending on the depth and the mineral content of the water feeding each individual lake. In spring light with the sun at a low angle, the shallow sections show the travertine bed clearly and the water seems to glow from below.

Plitvice Lakes National Park: UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1979. The park protects 296 sq km of the Mala Kapela and Plješevica mountain foothills, including the 16-lake system, 90+ waterfalls, and the old-growth beech-fir forest of Čorkova Uvala — one of the last primeval forests in Europe. The turquoise colour is produced by the specific mineral content (calcium carbonate, magnesium) of the water, combined with the light travertine bed. The colour cannot be replicated by filters — it is genuinely that shade.

Day 7 — The Lower Lakes & Veliki Slap
Friday, May 1 • Towards Croatia's Highest Waterfall

After crossing Lake Kozjak by electric ferry, the lower lake section presents an entirely different character: the valley widens, the falls grow larger, and the trail descends steadily toward the park's southern climax. The biggest waterfalls in the park are all in the lower section — the water has gathered volume over the 12 upper lakes and is now falling in earnest.

Veliki Slap, Croatia's highest waterfall at 78 metres, awaits at the southern end of the lower lake system. The approach reveals it gradually — you hear the thunder before you see it, then catch glimpses through the forest, then suddenly you're standing at the base looking up at 78 metres of continuous freefall. In April snowmelt, the volume is at its annual peak: the entire width of the fall is a solid white curtain, the plunge pool roiling below, the mist drifting back up the cliff face. We stood there for twenty minutes and nobody said much.

The lower lake boardwalks are the most photographed sections of the park — the wide cascades framed by dense forest on both sides, the turquoise pools below the falls, the mist softening everything slightly. The light in late afternoon catches the spray and creates constant partial rainbows. It is, by any objective measure, one of the most beautiful landscapes in Europe.

Veliki Slap: At 78 metres, Croatia's highest waterfall drops in a single unbroken column from the canyon rim above the Korana River gorge. The waterfall is fed by a karst spring above the rim rather than a surface stream — the water emerges through porous limestone rather than over a river lip. In peak spring flow (April–May), the width of the fall can exceed 20 metres. The Korana River gorge below the fall is itself dramatic: 40-metre limestone walls carved by the same river that created the lakes above.
